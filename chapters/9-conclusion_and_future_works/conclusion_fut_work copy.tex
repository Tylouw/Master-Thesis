\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec}



This thesis investigated robotic insertion-success classification using a real-world dataset collected with an industrial robot under varying insertion geometries, tolerances, gripper configurations, and process deviations. The main contribution is therefore not only the application of a time-series classification model, but the construction and analysis of a structured robotic insertion dataset. The model was primarily used as an analysis tool to evaluate how much information is contained in the recorded robot signals, how well insertion outcomes can be classified under different train-test splits, and which physical conditions are difficult for both the insertion process and the classifier.

The dataset consists of 8100 insertion attempts recorded over three batches. Each attempt was stored as a multivariate time series with 48 robot signals sampled at 500 Hz over a fixed sequence length of 3 s. In addition to the sensor data, each sample includes metadata describing the corresponding rod type, tolerance, gripper type, recording batch, applied position offset, applied tilt, insertion force level, force limit, record offset, binary success label, and failure reason. This metadata proved essential because it enabled analysis beyond global classification metrics and allowed the dataset to be studied with respect to its physical and domain-specific properties.

The channel study showed that insertion-success classification is not encoded in a single signal modality. Force/torque signals were the best individual modality, but the best performance was achieved by combining force/torque with TCP pose, joint velocity and current consumption. This indicates that robust classification benefits from diverse sensor information and confirms that the dataset contains meaningful multi-modal structure related to the insertion process.

The benchmark experiments demonstrated that classification difficulty strongly depends on the train-test split and the underlying domain shift. Benchmark 1 provided an in-distribution reference case and achieved the strongest overall performance, but this should not be interpreted as proof of generalization. Benchmarks 2 and 3 showed that the model generalized reasonably well across many unseen domains, especially between round geometries and tolerance variations, while rectangular geometries, rectangular grippers, and batch-level splits were more difficult. In particular, tight rectangular tolerances represented the most challenging domains. These results show that the dataset contains both transferable patterns and domain-specific effects that limit generalization.

The deviation analyses provided the clearest insight into the physical structure of the dataset. Deviation Analysis I showed that insertion success is mainly influenced by tilt magnitude and the interaction between insertion force level and force limit. Increasing tilt consistently reduced physical success, indicating that angular misalignment is more critical than the tested position offsets. The force-related analysis further showed that the relevant quantity is the difference between commanded force level and force limit. Small or negative differences strongly reduced success rates and produced different failure mechanisms such as force-limit failures, jams, or timeouts.

The metadata-based analysis also revealed local effects that would not be visible from global metrics alone. For example, the \texttt{small\_rod\_t0.3} domain showed directional asymmetries where negative offsets were less successful than positive ones, suggesting a bias in the nominal insertion pose. This demonstrates that the dataset is useful not only for training classifiers, but also for diagnosing weaknesses or asymmetries in the robotic setup itself.

Deviation Analysis II showed that physical difficulty and classification difficulty are related, but not identical. Large tilt reduced physical success rates, yet these groups often remained relatively easy to classify because they produced distinctive signal patterns. In contrast, force-interaction groups such as \texttt{F30/L30} and \texttt{F40/L40} caused both low balanced accuracy and high loss, indicating genuinely ambiguous or inconsistent dataset regions. A further important finding was that local physical success rates directly influence class balance. Physically easy or physically impossible groups create highly imbalanced local datasets, which can increase false positives or false negatives even when the physical behavior itself is consistent.

Overall, the thesis shows that the recorded insertion dataset contains rich and learnable structure, but that this structure is not uniform across all domains and deviation groups. Multi-modal robot signals enable reliable insertion-success classification under favorable and moderately shifted conditions, while rectangular contact geometries, batch-level shifts, unfavorable force interactions, and locally imbalanced groups reveal clear limits of generalization. The central insight is therefore not simply that insertion success can be classified, but that combining time-series classification with metadata-based analysis makes it possible to identify which parts of a robotic insertion dataset are physically difficult, difficult to classify, and where these two notions differ.


\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The results of this thesis have to be interpreted within the scope of the collected dataset and the experimental setup. The dataset was recorded on one robotic insertion system, with one task board, one robot, one sensor setup, and a defined set of rods, holes, tolerances, gripper conditions, and deviation settings. The results therefore show how insertion-success classification behaves for this specific experimental system. They do not directly prove that the same performance or the same difficult deviation groups, if the dataset would be recorded on another robot with different sensors, with another insertion task, different rod/hole geometries.

A second limitation is that the dataset is structured and diverse, but does not cover every possible combination of deviations and domains. Especially the relation between batch, rod type, and gripper type is not fully independent, because some gripper types only occur in specific batches or with specific rod types. In addition, Batch 1 was recorded with a different deviation strategy than Batches 2 and 3.

The deviation analyses also showed that global class balance is not sufficient to guarantee balanced local dataset regions. Although the complete dataset is approximately balanced between successful and failed insertions, several deviation groups are locally dominated by one class. This is not an error in the dataset, but a direct consequence of the physical insertion behavior. Physically easy conditions naturally produce mostly successful samples, while physically difficult conditions produce mostly failed samples. However, these local imbalances limit the interpretation of model performance in such groups, because rare successful or failed exceptions may be underrepresented. Heatmap cells or subgroups with low sample counts must therefore be interpreted carefully.

Another limitation concerns the labeling procedure. The binary success label and the failure reason were generated automatically during data collection using predefined thresholds and rule-based conditions. This ensured consistent labeling over all 8100 insertion attempts and avoided manual post-processing, but it also simplified the insertion process into discrete outcome classes. In reality, insertion quality can be more gradual. For example, two failed insertions with the same failure reason can still differ in contact behavior, severity, or recoverability. The failure-reason metadata is therefore useful for interpretation, but it does not fully capture all physical mechanisms occurring during an insertion.

The model evaluation was performed offline on fixed-length recorded sequences. The classifier therefore had access to a complete 3 s recording window and was not required to make an early decision during the insertion process. This is sufficient for analyzing the dataset and comparing domains, but it does not yet demonstrate that the classifier can be used for real-time monitoring, early failure prediction, or closed-loop recovery. Furthermore, the final class decisions depend on a selected decision threshold. Balanced accuracy was the main evaluation metric, while probability calibration was not optimized as a primary objective. This is visible in Benchmark 1, where the larger model achieved stronger classification performance but not the better loss behavior.

Finally, the thesis focused on an adapted PatchTST architecture with two main model configurations. This was sufficient to evaluate whether the dataset contains learnable time-series structure and to compare the influence of channels, domains, and deviations. However, the results do not prove that PatchTST is the optimal architecture for this task. Other model types or training strategies could behave differently, especially in locally imbalanced or domain-shifted regions of the dataset.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future_work}

Future work should first extend and refine the dataset. The most direct improvement would be to record additional batches under a more controlled and more independent experimental design. In particular, batch, rod type, tolerance, and gripper type should be decoupled as far as possible, so that the influence of each factor can be analyzed independently. A more complete factorial design would make it easier to distinguish whether a performance drop is caused by geometry, tolerance, gripper condition, batch-specific execution, or a combination of these effects.

A second important direction is targeted data collection for locally difficult and imbalanced deviation groups. The deviation analyses showed that physically extreme cases with almost only successes or almost only failures are problematic for classification, because rare exceptions are underrepresented. Future datasets should therefore deliberately include additional samples in these local regions. This does not mean that the robotic process should be made worse, but that the dataset should contain enough borderline and exception cases to study the transition between success and failure. Such targeted sampling would be especially useful for force-interaction groups such as \texttt{F30/L30}, \texttt{F40/L30}, and \texttt{F40/L40}, where the strongest model-confusion effects were observed.

The physical setup itself could also be improved based on the findings of the metadata analysis. The directional asymmetry observed in the \texttt{small\_rod\_t0.3} domain suggests a possible bias in the nominal insertion pose. Future work should correct this start pose and then repeat the corresponding measurements to verify whether the success-rate asymmetry is reduced. This would turn the dataset analysis into a direct improvement of the robotic process. In the same way, additional experiments could investigate whether the identified force-limit failure modes can be reduced by adapting force-control parameters or insertion strategies.

The labeling and metadata could be extended in future recordings. Instead of only using a binary success label, the dataset could include additional continuous or categorical information, such as insertion depth, maximum contact force, contact duration, recovery behavior, or manually validated failure modes. Video recordings or additional external sensors could help distinguish physical mechanisms that are not fully visible from the robot-internal data alone. This would make it possible to move from simple success/failure classification toward a more detailed diagnosis of insertion behavior.

On the model side, future work should investigate whether the observed dataset effects are specific to the adapted PatchTST model or whether they also occur with other architectures. Possible comparisons include convolutional networks, recurrent networks, smaller Transformer models, classical feature-based classifiers, or domain-adaptation methods. In addition, training strategies that explicitly address local class imbalance could be evaluated. Examples include domain-balanced sampling, subgroup-aware weighting, focal loss, or targeted augmentation of rare success and failure cases. These methods are especially relevant because the thesis showed that local class balance is one of the key factors influencing classification behavior.

Another useful extension would be a stronger focus on model interpretability. The current analysis identifies which deviation groups are difficult, but it does not fully explain which parts of the time series lead to the model decision. Future work could apply temporal attribution methods, channel-importance analysis, or saliency-based techniques to determine whether the model relies on physically plausible signal regions. This would help connect the classifier output more directly to contact events, force peaks, velocity changes, or actuator-load patterns during insertion.

Finally, the classifier should be evaluated in an online robotic setting. In this thesis, the model was used for offline classification of complete recorded sequences. A practical next step would be to classify partial sequences during the insertion process and determine how early a reliable success or failure prediction can be made. If successful, the model could be integrated into a closed-loop system that stops failed insertions earlier, triggers recovery motions, adapts the insertion strategy, or updates the robot pose based on predicted failure probability. This would extend the work from dataset analysis and offline classification toward active robotic insertion monitoring and recovery.
