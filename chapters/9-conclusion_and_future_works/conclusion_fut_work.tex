\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{ch:conclusion}
This chapter first concludes the thesis in Section \ref{sec:conclusion} by summarizing the dataset, the adapted PatchTST classification approach, the best channel study, the benchmark experiments, and the deviation analyses. The section then answers the research question.

Section \ref{sec:practical_implications} then derives practical recommendations for insertion setup design. These recommendations are divided according to two different objectives: maximizing the physical insertion success rate and maximizing classification performance. Finally, Section \ref{sec:limitations_future_work} discusses the main limitations of the robotic setup, dataset generation strategy, labeling approach, and offline classification evaluation, and outlines corresponding directions for future work.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This thesis investigated robotic insertion-success classification based on a real-world dataset collected with an UR5e industrial robot, multiple insertion geometries, tolerance levels, gripper configurations, and systematically deviations applied to the insertions. The central contribution of the work is therefore not only the application of a time-series classification model, but the construction and analysis of a structured robotic insertion dataset. The model was used as an analysis tool to evaluate how much information is contained in the recorded sensor signals, how well insertion outcomes can be classified under different data splits, and which physical deviations or dataset domains are difficult for both the insertion process and the classifier.

The collected dataset consists of 8100 insertions recorded over three batches. Each attempt was stored as a multivariate time series with 48 robot signals sampled at 500 Hz over a fixed sequence length of 3 s. In addition to the sensor data, each sample includes metadata describing the corresponding rod type, tolerance, gripper type, recording batch, applied position offset, applied tilt, insertion force level, force limit, record offset, binary success label, and failure reason. This metadata was essential for the later analysis, because it made it possible to not only do classification, but to also investigate the dataset with respect to its physical and domain-specific properties.

PatchTST was used as the main classification model throughout the experimental evaluation. The model transforms the multivariate time-series data into fixed-length patches, embeds these patches into tokens, and processes them with a Transformer-based architecture. For the binary insertion task, the model produces a single output logit, which is converted into a success probability and classified using a decision threshold.

The best channel study showed that insertion-success classification is not encoded in one signal group alone. Force/torque is the best singular modality, which is expected because it directly describes the contact interaction during insertion. However, the best results were achieved when force/torque was combined with additional modalities. The final selected channel configuration combines contact interaction through force/torque, robot state through TCP pose, motion dynamics through joint velocity and actuator load through current consumption. These channels are then used as input for PatchTST in combination with higher model capacity. This shows that robust classification benefits from diverse modalities rather than from one dominant channel group.

The benchmark experiments then showed how classification difficulty changes with the structure of the train/validation/test split. Benchmark 1 provided a favorable in-distribution reference case where training, validation and test samples were randomly drawn from the whole dataset. Under these conditions, the larger model configuration achieved the stronger balanced accuracy, but not necessarily the better prediction confidence. This result should therefore not be interpreted as proof of generalization to unseen physical conditions. Instead, it defines a baseline for how well the dataset can be classified in the best case.

Benchmark 2 and Benchmark 3 showed that the difficulty of the dataset is strongly domain-dependent. In Benchmark 2, the model generalized well to most individual unseen hole domains, but some geometry--tolerance combinations were clearly more difficult than others. Tighter tolerances generally reduced balanced accuracy, but this effect was more significant for rectangular holes than for round holes. The tightest rectangular domain therefore represents a particularly difficult case, where the combination of geometry and tolerance creates a more difficult scenario than tolerance alone. Benchmark 3 confirmed this tendency, by using larger held-out groups as the test set. The weakest results occurred for batch-level generalization and for rectangular contact conditions in general. These results show that the dataset contains both transferable patterns and domain-specific effects that limit generalization.

The deviation analyses provide the most direct insight into the physical structure of the dataset. Deviation Analysis I showed that physical insertion success is mainly influenced by tilt magnitude and the interaction between insertion force level and force limit. While position offset alone only varied on a relatively small scale, increasing tilt, consistently reduced the physical success rate. This means that angular misalignment is more impactful for the insertion process than radial position offset alone. The force-related results further showed that force level and force limit cannot be interpreted independently. The relevant quantity is the difference between force limit and commanded force level. If this difference is small or negative, the physical success rate decreases strongly, especially in settings such as \texttt{F40/L30}. The failure-reason analysis also showed that similar success rates can originate from different physical mechanisms. Low or negative force differences mainly lead to force-limit failures, larger force limits can allow more stall or jam failures, and low force levels can produce more timeouts.

The domain-specific deviation analysis revealed additional local effects that would not be visible from global metrics alone. In particular, the \texttt{small\_rod\_t0.3} domain showed that negative $x$ and negative $y$ offsets were less successful than positive offsets. This suggests a directional bias of the initial insertion pose for this specific hole. Such a finding is practically relevant because it does not only describe the dataset, but also points to a possible correction of the robotic setup by shifting the starting pose toward the true hole center. This finding proves the usefulness of the metadata-based analysis. The dataset is not only useful for training a classifier, but also for diagnosing physical weaknesses or asymmetries in the insertion process.

Deviation Analysis II showed that physical difficulty and classification difficulty are related, but not identical. Large tilt strongly reduced the physical success rate, but the corresponding large-tilt groups still achieved comparatively high balanced accuracy. This means that physically difficult insertions can still produce signal patterns that are clear and recognizable for the classifier. A deviation can therefore be unfavorable for the physical process without necessarily being confusing for the model. In contrast, the strongest model-confusion effects were found in the force-interaction groups. Settings with a small or negative difference between force limit and force level reduced balanced accuracy, and groups such as \texttt{F30/L30} and \texttt{F40/L40} showed both low balanced accuracy and high loss. These groups are therefore not only misclassified after thresholding, but also contain signal patterns for which the model was confidently wrong. This indicates genuinely ambiguous or inconsistent local dataset regions.

A central finding of the thesis is that the physical success rate of a deviation group directly affects the local class balance available for training and evaluation. From a robotic industrial perspective, a success rate close to 100\% is desirable and a success rate close to 0\% is undesirable. From a classification perspective, however, both extremes are problematic because they create highly imbalanced classes. In these cases, the model tends to learn the dominant outcome and struggles with rare exceptions. Conversely, a physical success rate around 50\% is not desirable for the real insertion process, but provides a more balanced class distribution for the classifier. This explains why physically easy groups can still show increased false-positive behavior and physically difficult groups can show increased false-negative behavior. The classification difficulty of a dataset region therefore depends not only on the physical difficulty of the insertion, but also on how many successful and failed examples are present.

Overall, the thesis shows that the recorded insertion dataset contains rich and learnable structure, but that this structure is not uniform across all domains and deviation groups. Multi-modal robot signals make reliable insertion-success classification possible under favorable and moderately shifted conditions. At the same time, rectangular contact geometries, batch-level shifts, unfavorable force interactions, and locally imbalanced deviation groups reveal clear limits of the classifier.
% The biggest insight is therefore not simply that a model can classify insertion success, but that the combination of time-series classification and metadata-based deviation analysis can identify which parts of a robotic insertion dataset are physically difficult, which parts are difficult to classify, and where these two differ. This makes the dataset itself the central result of the thesis, because it provides a structured basis for studying robotic insertion processes, machine-learning classification performance, and the relationship between physical process variation and data-driven model behavior.

\section{Practical Recommendations for Insertion Setup Design}
\label{sec:practical_implications}

The results of this thesis also lead to practical recommendations for designing robotic insertion setups, depending on whether the main objective is high physical success rate or high classification performance. These two objectives are related, but they do not necessarily lead to the same experimental design.

If the goal is to maximize the physical insertion success rate, the following recommendations should be considered by descending priority:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The setup should minimize angular misalignment. Across the dataset, tilt was the strongest global factor reducing insertion success and should therefore be prioritized before smaller position-offset corrections.
    \item The force interaction should be chosen such that the difference between force limit and commanded force level remains sufficiently large. A small or negative force difference increases the probability of force-limit failures and should be avoided for robust insertion.
    \item Round insertion geometries should be preferred over rectangular contact conditions where possible, because they showed stronger generalization behavior and fewer difficult contact cases.
    \item Looser tolerances improve the physical success probability, although this may conflict with application requirements.
    \item The start pose should be carefully centered. The directional asymmetries observed in the dataset indicate that even a small bias in the nominal insertion pose can reduce success rates in specific domains.
\end{enumerate}

If a high physical insertion success rate cannot be achieved reliably, a classifier becomes practically relevant as a monitoring layer. In this case, the objective is no longer only to make the insertion succeed, but also to ensure that failed insertions can be detected reliably so that the system can stop the process, trigger a recovery strategy, or reject the part. For an operational setup, the highest possible physical success rate should still be maintained. However, the training and validation data for the classifier must contain sufficiently informative success and failure examples. That means only for the duration of data gathering, the setup most produce a lot of failure cases.

If the goal is to build such a dataset or to tune the setup for high classification performance, the following recommendations should be considered by descending priority:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The training dataset should be locally balanced with respect to the relevant deviation groups. A physical success rate close to 50\% is not desirable for an operational insertion system, but it is useful for training and evaluating a classifier because both successful and failed insertions must be sufficiently represented. In contrast, groups with almost only successful or almost only failed insertions can lead the model to learn the dominant class and miss rare exceptions.

\item Failure cases should be generated by deviations that produce recognizable signal patterns rather than ambiguous behavior. Large tilt is the most suitable deviation for this purpose, because it strongly reduces physical success while remaining informative for classification. In contrast, adding position offset to large tilt did not provide a meaningful additional reduction of success rate or improvement of classification performance in this dataset and is therefore not necessary as a primary mechanism for generating classifiable failures.

\item Force-interaction settings should be selected carefully. The objective is not to create arbitrary failures, but failures with consistent and distinguishable signal patterns. In the thesis dataset, \texttt{F25/L50} and \texttt{F35/L50} are recommended settings, because they combine physical success rates of 0.47 and 0.53 with balanced accuracies of approximately 0.95 and 0.92, respectively. In contrast, settings with a small or negative force difference, such as \texttt{F30/L30}, \texttt{F40/L30}, and \texttt{F40/L40}, should be avoided for this purpose because they can create rare exceptions or ambiguous signal patterns that reduce classification performance.

\item The recording should include the most informative signal groups identified in the channel study. Force/torque should be included as the main contact-related modality, but TCP pose, joint velocity, and current consumption should also be recorded because they provide additional information about robot state, motion dynamics, and actuator load.

\end{enumerate}



\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations_future_work}

The limitations of this thesis mainly concern the robotic setup, the dataset generation strategy, and the scope of the classification evaluation. Since the dataset was recorded on one specific robotic insertion setup with one robot, one task board, and a fixed sensor configuration, the results describe the behavior of this experimental system and should not be directly generalized to all robotic insertion tasks. In addition, the current setup represents insertion through a plate-like hole geometry, where contact is limited to the thickness of the plate. Future work could extend the setup with a deeper cylindrical guide or sleeve, creating a more realistic peg-in-hole or shaft-in-bore contact.

A second limitation is that the dataset is diverse, but not fully independent across all experimental factors. Rod type, tolerance, gripper type, and recording batch are partly coupled, which makes it difficult to point domain shifts to one isolated cause. This is especially relevant for the batch-level results, where sample composition, deviation strategy, and execution variations all contribute to the observed performance. Future development could therefore decouple these factors as far as possible, so that the effect of each domain variable can be analyzed more independently.

The deviation planner described in Section \ref{sec:dataset_collection} also limited the dataset structure. Although the planner was deterministic and reproducible, its modulo-based selection of deviation levels was too rigid and static. Since deviation pools of different lengths were cycled periodically, some deviation levels did not interact equally with others. Future versions of the planner should keep reproducibility, but introduce controlled shuffling of deviation levels within each repeated block. The sampling window per main configuration could also be increased beyond the current 40 insertions for tight tolerances and 30 insertions for the other tolerance levels. At the same time, factors with minor influence, such as grasp height and approach height, should not dominate the outer loop structure of the recording plan. More samples should instead be assigned to the deviations that were shown to be physically and classification relevant, especially tilt and force interaction.

The labeling approach could also be extended. The current binary success label and rule-based failure reasons provide a consistent basis for classification and analysis, but they simplify the insertion process into discrete outcomes. Future work could include more detailed labels or continuous process descriptors, such as insertion depth, contact duration, maximum contact force, or manually validated failure modes. This would allow the dataset to be used not only for success classification, but also for a more detailed diagnosis of insertion behavior.

Finally, the classification results are limited to offline evaluation with the adapted PatchTST model. The model classifies complete recorded sequences and was not evaluated as an online predictor during insertion. Future work could therefore investigate whether reliable predictions can already be made from without the full view on a sequence, which would be necessary for early failure detection. In addition, PatchTST could be compared with other time-series classifiers discussed in Chapter \ref{ch:state-of-the-art}, such as HIVE-COTE 2.0, ROCKET, InceptionTime, and other convolutional or transformer-based approaches. This would help distinguish dataset-related difficulty from specific model architectures. Further analysis could be done to identify which parts or local patterns of the time series specifically cause correct or incorrect predictions and physical success or fail.
